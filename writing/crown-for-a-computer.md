# Crown for a Computer

*A crown of sonnets. Each poem's last line is the next poem's first; the last line of the seventh is the first line of the first.*

---

## I

She has not seen the sea. She knows the sea
as figures: heights of water, hours, in feet
and minutes, at a harbour wall where she
has never stood, set down in rows as neat
as print; ruled columns, one for every pull
that works the water: here the moon's; and here
the sun's; and here the moon when it is full
and near; and here its leaning through the year.
She adds them: twelve slow motions, each one true
to its own clock, and none in step with none,
make one plain figure—nothing in it new,
and nothing like it ever, under the sun.
She rules it off. The rows run on for months,
and every tide is many tides at once.

## II

And every tide is many tides at once:
the moon's, which comes twice daily and comes late
by fifty minutes every day; the sun's,
exact as railways. Twice a month their weight
falls in together, and the water heaps
against the harbour steps: the springs. And when
they pull at odds, the water barely creeps:
the neaps. Add that the moon comes close, and then
swings wide; and that it leans; and that the sun
leans too, across the year. None of them keep
another's time; no pattern, once begun,
comes back entire. And nothing of it sleeps.
She writes the hour, the height: so many feet.
The harbour fills for men she'll never meet.

## III

The harbour fills for men she'll never meet,
whose names she will not learn, as they'll not learn
hers—there's no name upon the printed sheet.
A master waiting for the tide to turn
reads it and trusts it as he trusts the stone
beneath his boots. He doesn't ask whose hand
has written it. One doesn't. Who is known
to stand behind the moon? The figures stand
for it. He crosses: fourteen feet of keel
on sixteen feet of water, and he's through,
and does not think of her, and does not feel
how thin it was. She does. She always knew
how much a pencil holds above the ground:
a minute's error is a ship aground.

## IV

A minute's error is a ship aground.
Each winter, when the harbour office sends
the gauge's record back, she goes around
her columns with it: where her figure ends,
the water's starts. And mostly they agree
within a pencil's width. But year by year
something is off, by less than one could see
from any quay: the water's not quite here,
where she has put it—it's a shade too low,
and lower each winter. She works the sums
again. The moon is right. The sun. And so
the sky is not at fault. And then it comes:
the fault is in the zero, in the sill.
The ground itself was never standing still.

## V

The ground itself was never standing still.
Pressed under ice an age ago, the land
is rising yet, the way a pillow will
once the head's lifted. And the mark, the hand-
cut zero on the harbour sill, goes up
with it, so that the water, answering
to nothing but the moon, appears to drop.
She finds the rate. A marginal note: "Bring
the datum down by so much every year,"
and writes the constants fair, and tells no one.
No one to tell. The pilots, next spring, clear
the bar and never know what she has done,
or that the wall was climbing by degrees.
What holds is mostly what nobody sees.

## VI

What holds is mostly what nobody sees.
She walks home in the dark. Under her coat
the heart does what it does and will not cease,
and never asked her; and the breath, by rote,
comes in and goes; her blood keeps its own tide
that runs loose time against the moon, the way
the harbour's does, a little late, or wide
of where it should be, never twice. The day
goes out of her like water off the steps,
and that is sleep, which is a tide as well;
and in the dark it turns, and something creeps
back up the stone, and morning, on the swell,
carries her up the hill, and the lamp burns.
Nothing returns the same, and all returns.

## VII

Nothing returns the same, and all returns.
The moon comes back a thumb's width further out
each year—it's leaving, slowly, and she learns
this from a paper, and will never doubt
or see it: not a second's worth in all
her life. The water comes back to the stair
and is not the same water; and the wall
it climbs has risen, slightly; and the chair
she sits in is the chair, but she is not
quite who sat in it yesterday: one more
day in her, one more page. If one could plot
the whole of it from far enough—the store
of days—there'd be one shape she'll never see.
She has not seen the sea. She knows the sea.

### Afterword

Before the word meant a machine, a *computer* was a person who computed. By the 1890s many of them were women, working at desks in observatories and almanac offices, and some of what they computed was tide tables. The tide at a harbour is the sum of a dozen or more cosine waves, each driven by one piece of the sky: the moon twice a day, the sun twice a day, the moon's elliptical orbit, the tilt of the moon's path, the tilt of the sun's. Kelvin worked out the method in the 1860s and George Darwin refined it; a machine to do the summing existed by the 1870s, but the constants for each port still had to be found by hand from years of gauge records, and a great deal of the arithmetic was still done by people, for harbours they would never visit.

The other facts in the poem are real. The moon is receding from the earth at a few centimetres a year, and the days are lengthening by it; Darwin published the mechanism in 1879, and nobody alive will ever see the effect. Scotland and Scandinavia, pressed down by ice sheets that melted ten thousand years ago, are still rising, a few millimetres a year, so that a tide gauge fixed to the land will slowly read the sea as falling. The pilots who read a table do not, as a rule, know who made it.

A crown of sonnets is a closed loop that never retraces its steps: the same line returns, but it means something else when it comes back. I wrote this one in the same sitting as a set of pictures of strange attractors, which are the same idea drawn instead of said, and I don't think either would have come out the way it did without the other.
